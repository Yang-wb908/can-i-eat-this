Food taboos in Islam, briefly

Islamic law sorts actions into five rulings. Food mostly sits at the two ends, with a wide doubtful band between them.

What the Qur’an names

Four things are forbidden by name: carrion, blood, the flesh of swine, and what is dedicated to other than God (2:173, 5:3). Intoxicants are called “defilement from the work of Satan” (5:90). Everything else starts from permission: “eat of what is on earth, lawful and good” (2:168).

Slaughter

Permitted animals become halal meat only through proper slaughter with God’s name pronounced (6:121). The food of the People of the Book, Jews and Christians, is also permitted (5:5). Whether that covers industrial meat in a country like Korea is a live debate.

Doubtful matters

“The lawful is clear and the unlawful is clear, and between them are doubtful matters” (Bukhari 52). Food whose source cannot be known is called mashbooh. Many Muslims avoid it; others ask first. Korean stocks, sauces and seasoning powders fall here constantly.

Necessity

The same verse that forbids pork adds: “whoever is forced by necessity, neither desiring it nor exceeding the limit, there is no sin on him” (2:173). This principle, darura, means the taboo bends when real hunger or danger leaves no other choice.

Schools disagree

The four Sunni schools and the Ja‘fari school agree on pork and wine but split on the sea. Most permit all sea creatures (5:96). The Hanafi school limits sea food to fish, and Ja‘fari law allows scaled fish and shrimp. One jar of salted shrimp can therefore get three different answers.

Personal and social

For the individual, the taboo is a daily act of devotion and identity: every meal is a small decision. For the community, it marks belonging and creates shared spaces such as halal restaurants and mosque kitchens. In a non-Muslim society it can also become a source of distance at shared tables.

How it works

Three steps, all inside your browser. First the dish is identified: typed names are matched to 100 Korean dishes by English name, romanization and near-misspellings, and photos go to a small image model that runs on your device. Then the ingredients come from the recipe set stored in the page, each marked with how often it appears and whether it can be seen. Finally the team’s rule table and your settings turn those ingredients into a verdict.

A dish outside the set is estimated from the words in its name: named ingredients (“pork”, “shrimp”, “wine”), dish types that hint at hidden ones (a stew implies stock, a cutlet implies frying oil), and any known Korean dish inside the name (“bulgogi pizza”). These estimates are marked low confidence.

The photo model

SigLIP, an open image–text model, compressed to 4 bits (about 60 MB, downloaded once and then cached). It was not trained on these dishes. Each dish is described in a sentence, and the photo is matched to the closest description. On 357 freely licensed photos of 97 of the dishes from Wikimedia Commons, its first guess was right 52% of the time and the right dish was in its top three 76% of the time, so the page always shows three candidates and lets you pick.

Photos never leave your device.

Limits

It describes a typical recipe, not the kitchen in front of you. A photo cannot show stock, marinade or frying oil, and the photo model only knows the 100 dishes in the set. Blood is also forbidden (2:173); in this set it appears only in pork-based sundae and is counted under pork. Presets follow common positions of each school, and individual scholars differ.

This site does not replace halal certification or a scholar’s answer.

AI policy

No outside AI service is called. The only model is the photo model, and it does one job: naming the dish. It never sees the rule table and never makes a ruling. The ingredient profiles were drafted with AI help and are being checked by the team.
